# Block screen copy

The shield appears when someone tries to enter a blocked app. Its copy should
help them move on before starting a session, without assuming they are already
scrolling. Its voice is
direct, confident, and respectful. It does not assume the user is distressed,
needs reassurance, or should turn the pause into a productive activity.

The three messages about time gained and ownership of attention are retained
verbatim based on user preference. Broad claims about possible focus, sleep,
and stress benefits were too abstract to help at the moment of opening an
app. The final set addresses that moment directly: reclaiming time, resisting
the urge to enter the app, and choosing where attention goes. The user's
preference for direct language about reclaiming time sets the voice.

## Evidence reviewed

- **Attention and connection:** Castelo et al., *PNAS Nexus* (2025), randomized
  participants to two weeks of blocked mobile internet. The intervention
  improved objectively measured sustained attention, subjective well-being,
  and mental health. Participants could still call, text, and use the internet
  on other devices. Compliance was difficult: 119 of 467 people who committed
  met the preregistered adherence threshold. Blocking selected apps is a
  different intervention, so the shield makes no claim about the magnitude
  or timing of an individual's improvement.
  [Paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11834938/)
- **Sleep and stress:** Pieh et al., *BMC Medicine* (2025), randomized 111 healthy
  students to three weeks of reduced smartphone use (a target of at most two
  hours daily) or a control group. The reduction group showed small to medium
  improvements in sleep, stress, well-being, and depressive symptoms.
  Screen time rebounded after the intervention. This supports discussing
  possible benefits of sustained reductions; it does not establish that a
  short pause immediately improves sleep or stress.
  [Paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11846175/)
- **Benefits vary with the intervention:** Walsh et al., *PLOS ONE* (2024),
  randomized 338 participants to eight days of digital-media restriction,
  social-media restriction, or controls. Broad digital restriction produced
  several well-being benefits; social-media restriction produced fewer
  benefits and some potential costs. Less screen time is not universally
  better, and the value and context of use matter.
  [Paper](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0306910)
- **Concrete messages warrant consideration:** Grummon et al., *JAMA Health
  Forum* (2026), tested messages with 1,012 participants aged 13–29. Specific
  health warnings generally received higher perceived-effectiveness ratings
  than a generic screen-time-break message. The addiction warning did not
  significantly outperform the break message. Outcomes were ratings, not
  observed behavior. The study tested warnings, not this shield's positive
  wording. Using concrete benefits here is an editorial inference, not a
  demonstrated effect of these exact sentences.
  [Paper](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama-health-forum/fullarticle/2853321)
- **A check can lead to more use:** Oulasvirta et al., *Personal and Ubiquitous
  Computing* (2012), describe repeated checking of quickly accessible dynamic
  content. A controlled field experiment found that informational rewards
  reinforced checking, and the studies found that checking sometimes prompted
  further activities on the device. This supports addressing the transition
  from a brief check to continued use. It does not establish a typical session
  length or imply that every check becomes a long session.
  [Paper](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00779-011-0412-2)
- **A stopping point can be chosen:** Natarajan, *International Journal of
  Communication* (2024), interviewed 20 teenagers aged 13–16 about stopping
  social media use. The paper situates participants' self-created frictions
  within designs that remove stopping cues. This qualitative work provides
  background on deliberate friction; it does not test this shield or establish
  a causal effect of its copy.
  [Paper](https://ijoc.org/index.php/ijoc/article/view/21618)
- **An urge need not dictate an action:** VA urge-surfing guidance teaches
  noticing cravings and their changes without automatically acting. The aim
  is to experience the urge differently, not to require it to disappear first.
  This supports a pause that does not depend on making an urge disappear.
  The final copy keeps the language about time and choice. The guidance does
  not establish that a shield message treats addiction.
  [Guidance](https://www.mirecc.va.gov/MIRECC/visn5/EBT/CBT-SUD/Urge-Surfing.asp)

## Final messages

| Message | Basis |
| --- | --- |
| Your attention is yours to spend. | Agency; editorial language rather than a scientific claim. |
| Reclaim your time. Start with this moment. | An immediate action framed as ownership of time, reflecting the user's preferred direction. |
| Resist the urge. | User-selected, direct encouragement at the attempt to enter a blocked app. It does not assume a scrolling session has begun. |
| Take back your time. | User-selected emphasis on ownership of time, without implying a specific session length. |
| 20 minutes less a day frees up over 5 days a year. | Arithmetic: 20 × 365 = 7,300 minutes, or about 5.07 full 24-hour days. This is time available for other things, not measured usage reduction or a health threshold. |
| The next few minutes are yours to choose. | An immediate, concrete choice; editorial language. |

The exact wording is an editorial decision informed by the research and user
preferences; these sentences have not been experimentally validated. The
messages do not diagnose addiction, prescribe a screen-time threshold, predict
feelings, or claim a dopamine reset. The health studies above provide background
and do not establish effects of individual app dismissals.

Only one message appears at a time, separated from the factual block details.
Selection stays stable within each 15-minute window across apps. There is no
attempt tracking, escalation, or assumption about a user's past behavior.
